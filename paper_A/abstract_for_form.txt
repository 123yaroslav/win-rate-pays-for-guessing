Models and training data are often compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what win rate pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with keys, six judges that give a short verdict preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. In a control added later, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% against the right answer in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong, and they still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy. A changed instruction repaired 3 of 6 judges, and reasoning before the verdict removed most of the preference in two of three. Outside the duel, a judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered test on fine-tuned models failed because no model abstained, and a follow-up with longer training showed the loss of abstention in the trained model. A deployed data pipeline replaced 99 of 100 abstentions with answers, 7 of them wrong, and its quality grade rose from C to A. We propose reporting, next to any win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
