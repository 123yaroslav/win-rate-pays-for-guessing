Models and training data are often compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what win rate pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with keys, six judges that give a bare verdict preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. In a control added later, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% against the right answer in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong, and they still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy. No single change of instruction repaired more than 2 of 6 judges, and a written rationale before the verdict left the preference in place for four. A judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered test on fine-tuned models failed, and a follow-up showed that a trained model abstains about as often as its training data, whoever selected it. A deployed data pipeline replaced 99 of 100 abstentions with answers on exam questions and kept them on questions it could not know. We propose reporting, next to any win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
