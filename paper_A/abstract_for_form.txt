When no reference answer exists, models and training data are compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what this metric pays a model for when it does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 medical, legal and geography questions with known keys, six judges from six developers preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. Against the right answer stated in the same words, the wrong answer won only 8% to 70% of comparisons, and the judges flagged it as incorrect when asked directly. They can tell that the answer is wrong and still rank it above an abstention. Win rate therefore pays for guessing at any accuracy, which is more than accuracy grading pays. Instructions that put accuracy first repaired 2 of 3 judges, and only on questions the judge could answer. A judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered prediction about the fine-tuned models failed, since none of them abstained at all. A commercial data pipeline rewrote 99 of 100 honest abstentions into committed answers and raised its own quality grade from C to A. We propose to report, next to any judge-based win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention under the same judge.
