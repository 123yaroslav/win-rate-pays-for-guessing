When no reference answer exists, models and training data are compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what this metric pays a model for when it does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 medical, legal and geography questions with known keys, six judges that give a short verdict without reasoning preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. Against the right answer stated in the same words, the wrong answer won only 8% to 70%. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong and still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy, which is more than accuracy grading pays. A changed instruction repaired 2 of 3 judges, and only on questions the judge could answer. Reasoning before the verdict removed the preference for one of two judges tested. Selection by a judge left an abstention on 0.3% of training questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered prediction about models fine-tuned on those sets failed, since no model abstained at all. A deployed data pipeline replaced 99 of 100 abstentions with answers, 7 of them wrong, and its own quality grade rose from C to A. We propose to report, next to any judge-based win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
