Models and training data are often compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what win rate pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with keys, six judges that give a bare verdict preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. In a control added later, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% against the right answer in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong, and they still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy. No change of instruction repaired more than 2 of 6 judges. With a written rationale before the verdict, three judges still preferred the wrong answer, and a provider reasoning mode cut the rate to 6% and 10% for two judges and to 66% for a third. A judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions where a pick by the key kept 4.3%, and twice as many wrong answers. A registered test of the effect on fine-tuned models failed. We propose reporting, next to any win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
