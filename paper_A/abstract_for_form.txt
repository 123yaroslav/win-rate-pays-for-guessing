Models and training data are often compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what it pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with keys, six judges that give a short verdict without reasoning preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly. In a control added later, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% against the right answer in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong and rank it above an abstention, so win rate pays for guessing at any accuracy. A changed instruction repaired 2 of 6 judges. Reasoning before the verdict cut the preference to 6% and 10% for two judges and to 66% for a third. A judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered test on fine-tuned models failed, since no model abstained. With longer training, a model trained on unselected cautious answers abstained on 17.3% of test questions and one trained on judge-selected answers on none. A deployed data pipeline replaced 99 of 100 abstentions with answers, 92 right and 7 wrong, and its quality grade rose from C to A. We propose reporting, next to any win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
