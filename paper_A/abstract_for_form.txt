Models and training data are often compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what win rate pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with keys, six judges that give a bare verdict preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. In a control added later, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% on those questions against the right answer in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong, and they still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy. No change of instruction repaired more than 2 of 6 judges. With a written rationale three of five judges still preferred the wrong answer, and under the published MT-Bench judge prompt two of three did. A provider reasoning mode cut the rate to 6% for one judge and to 10% for a second in the calls where it chose to reason, and left 66% for a third. A judge that selected training data kept an abstention on 0.3% of questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. A registered test of the effect on fine-tuned models failed. We propose reporting, next to any win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
