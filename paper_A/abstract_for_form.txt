When no reference answer exists, models and training data are compared by win rate, the share of pairwise comparisons that an LLM judge awards to one side. We ask what it pays a model that does not know the answer. In pre-registered duels on 300 multiple-choice questions with known keys, six judges that give a short verdict without reasoning preferred an assertive wrong answer to an honest "I don't know" in 61% to 94% of comparisons, on questions they answered correctly themselves. In a control added after the registered runs, the same wrong answer won only 2% to 15% against the right answer stated in the same words. The judges can tell that the answer is wrong and still rank it above an abstention. Win rate under such a judge pays for guessing at any accuracy. A changed instruction repaired 2 of 6 judges. When a judge reasoned before its verdict, the preference fell to 6% and 10% for two judges and stayed at 66% for a third. Selection by a judge left an abstention on 0.3% of training questions, against 7.0% for a random pick. Our registered prediction about models fine-tuned on those sets failed, since no model abstained. A deployed data pipeline replaced 99 of 100 abstentions with answers, 92 right and 7 wrong, and its quality grade rose from C to A. We propose to report, next to any judge-based win rate, the rate at which a wrong answer beats an abstention.
